How it works

Solving Jumbleberry Fields, step by step

"Solved" means I know the best possible move in every situation the game can produce, and exactly how much each move is worth. Here is how a computer works that out, why this game solves in under a second while Yahtzee needs hundreds of millions of positions, and how I checked the answer.

1 · Count the game

Fewer situations than it looks

Five jars, each showing one of five things, can land 3,125 ways. But only how many of each berry you have matters, not which jar holds which. That leaves just 126 different hands. Every move you make is a lock, meaning a set of berries to keep, and there are only 252 different locks. Nine boxes that are each open or used give 512 scorecards.

126different hands of 5 berries
252different locks you can make
512scorecards (each box open or used)
193,536decision points: 512 scorecards × 3 rolls × 126 hands

The points already on your card don't change your best move, because Jumbleberry Fields has no bonuses. Only which boxes are still open matters for the future. That is the big shortcut. Yahtzee needs to remember your top-section total for its 35-point bonus and whether you can still earn Yahtzee bonuses, which is why Ballpark Figures' Yahtzee solve had to work through about 405 million positions.

2 · Work backwards

Start at the last roll of the game

Planning forward is hopeless: every choice branches into hundreds of futures. So the solver starts at the end, where the answer is obvious, and steps backwards one decision at a time. A round looks like this, and the solver fills in every box from right to left:

start of roundValue of the scorecardAverage of all 126 first rolls
roll 1See a hand126 possible hands
choicePick a lockAverage over every way the re-rolled jars land
roll 2 → choicePick a lock againSame idea, one roll left
roll 3 → choicePick a boxPoints now + value of the scorecard that's left

The last round. Suppose only Free Roll is left. After your third roll there's nothing to decide. After your second roll, the solver tries every lock. For each one it lists every way the other jars can land, multiplies each result by its chance, and adds them up. The lock with the highest average wins: keep Pickleberries and Moonberries, re-roll the rest. One roll earlier it does the same, using those averages as the payoff. Averaged over first rolls, a lone Free Roll is worth 19.4 points.

Every earlier round works the same way, with one addition. When you pick a box, the payoff is the points you score now plus the value of the scorecard that's left, which the solver has already worked out because it is going backwards. After 511 scorecards it reaches the empty card: 121.80 points on average with perfect play.

value(card) = average over first rolls of best lock → average over re-rolls of best lock → average over re-rolls of best box: points now + value(card with that box used)

In code this is about 30 lines (solver.py, and jbf-engine.js for the browser). It runs in under a tenth of a second.

4 · Playing for prizes

When the score you need matters

Maximising the average ignores the prize lines. To play for them, the solver needs one more fact: how many points you've banked. Anything above 141 counts the same, so the banked score only needs 142 values (0 to 141). That turns 512 scorecards into about 73,000. The payoff at the end is no longer points but Moonberries: 1 for 121 to 140 and 2 for 141 or more. Running the same backwards process gives the best achievable chance of each prize from any position, and the strategy that wins the most Moonberries over time: 0.67 a game. It takes about a second.

This is the part a Yahtzee solve usually leaves out. Beating a target is a different goal from scoring the most on average, and here I could solve it exactly.

6 · Checking the answer

How I know it's right

  • Simulation agrees. 200,000 simulated games played with the solver's moves averaged 121.81, against the exact 121.80. The prize odds matched to a tenth of a percent.
  • Two independent versions agree. The Python solver and the browser version give the same values to four decimal places.
  • Scoring is tested. Automated tests check every one of the 126 hands in every box against the original game code.
  • Learning machines agree. A neural network that taught itself from 6 million practice games, never told the odds, ended up within 0.8 points of the solver.
