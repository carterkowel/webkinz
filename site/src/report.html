What is Jumbleberry Fields?

Jumbleberry Fields is a nostalgic arcade game on Webkinz. It's basically a simpler version of Yahtzee (also nostalgic): roll five jars of berries up to three times a round, keep the ones you want, and fill a scorecard of nine boxes.

Why it feels hard

The Moonberry line sits right on the perfect player's average

The prize for one Moonberry starts at 121. A flawless player averages 121.8. So even perfect play is close to a coin flip for the first Moonberry, and two Moonberries (141+) come in roughly one game in nine. If the top prize feels out of reach, that is mostly the dice.

How strategies stack up

Which box you score matters as much as what you lock

Each simulated strategy played the same 200,000 games. "Points lost" grades the decisions themselves: for every move it counts how many points, on average, it gives up compared with the best move. That separates skill from luck.

Random locking scores no better than never re-rolling at all. Locking the biggest matching group, the usual Yahtzee instinct, only reaches 79. The jump from about 109 to about 120 comes from which box each hand gets scored in, which is what the par sheet below fixes.

What perfect play does

Habits of a perfect player

These patterns come from watching the solver play millions of rounds. Each one held up across every situation I checked.

Locking

  • Lock Moonberries.Perfect play keeps them 84% of the time, and 96% while the Moonberry box is open.
  • Lock Pickleberries, especially early.Kept 77% of the time while the Pickleberry box is open.
  • Never lock a Fly.Not once, in any situation.
  • Throw back pairs of Jumble or Sugar.A pair is kept only 13% of the time. Three of a kind is kept 62% of the time, four 91%.

Scoring

  • Basket of 5 is the throw-away box.Perfect play leaves it at zero 45% of the time. Jumbleberry and Sugarberry almost never get a zero (0.4%).
  • Three Moonberries go in the Moonberry box.MMMJJ scores 21 there and 25 in Basket of 3. Take the 21: Basket of 3 is easy to fill later, a 21 in Moonberry is not.
  • Five Jumble or Sugar go in Basket of 5.It's the only time that box pays, so use it.
  • Take Mixed Basket and Basket of 3 when they come.They're usually the first boxes filled, around round 4.
  • Don't waste Free Roll.It has the highest par on the sheet. Spend it on a big total or to rescue a round, not on an ordinary hand.

Rolling

  • Use all three rolls.Perfect play stops early in only about 11% of rounds.
  • Don't chase a Mixed Basket in round 1.With JSPMF lock only the Pickleberry and Moonberry. It keeps more boxes in play.
  • Pick a box to chase, then lock for it.The par sheet tells you which box is worth chasing.

The par sheet

Nine numbers that get you within 2 points of perfect

A par is what an open box is worth to you. When a round ends, score your hand in the box where points minus par is biggest. A bad round goes where it hurts least compared with par.

  1. Each roll, choose one open box to chase: the one where you can most likely beat its par from the berries you have.
  2. Lock the berries that help that box, and re-roll the rest.
  3. After the last roll, score the hand where points minus par is highest. Five Sugarberries are 10 points. That is 0 against par in the Sugarberry box but +4 in Basket of 5, so Basket of 5 wins.

Four examples

The first is a clear call. The others are where instinct and the par sheet disagree. Every example matches what the exact solver does in that spot.

Played this way the par sheet averages 119.8, 98% of perfect play, and reaches 121 in 48% of games. Both habits that matter most are built in: Jumbleberry and Sugarberry are valued at their maximum, so they are never used as dumps, and Basket of 5 is cheap.

Playing for prizes

Which goal wins the most Moonberries?

Scoring 121 to 140 wins one Moonberry and 141 or more wins two. If you play every day, what counts is the total number of Moonberries you collect. I solved for that directly: a strategy that maximises Moonberries won per game, counting the second one at 141.

What you play forMoonberries per 100 games121+141+Average

Play for the most Moonberries. In practice that means playing for 121 first. Once 121 looks likely with rounds to spare, it starts taking risks for 141. It gives up half a percentage point on the first Moonberry (57.4% instead of 57.9%) to lift the second from 5.9% to 9.3%. That adds about 3 Moonberries per 100 games over playing for 121 alone. The helper plays this way by default.

If you have to pick one fixed goal, pick 1 Moonberry. Always chasing 141 wins about 38% fewer Moonberries over time. The extra two-Moonberry games don't make up for all the games that miss 121.

Can an AI learn it?

Teaching machines to play, and grading them

Because perfect play is known exactly, every player can be graded move by move. I trained two learning AIs that start knowing nothing about the odds and improve by playing. I also asked Claude Opus 5.5, Anthropic's AI model, to play using only the rules: no solver, no code, just its own reasoning.

Its costliest habit is one many players share: spending Basket of 3 on modest hands. Examples: 17 points from PJJMJ on a first roll, or 12 from four Jumbleberries. Perfect play saves Basket of 3 for big totals (it averages about 21 there) and often puts three or four Jumbleberries or Sugarberries in their own box.

Solving the game it's built on

Jumbleberry Fields is a berry-themed cousin of Yahtzee, and Yahtzee has been solved with the same technique. For a video on that, watch I Solved Yahtzee* by Ballpark Figures.

Yahtzee is the bigger puzzle. It has 13 boxes instead of 9, a 35-point bonus for the top section and extra Yahtzee bonuses, so a solver has to track running totals as well as which boxes are open. Jumbleberry Fields has no bonuses, which is why it can be solved in under a second. See how I did it, step by step.
